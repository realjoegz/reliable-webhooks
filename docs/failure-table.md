# Failure table

Each row names one failure, what the service does about it and the test that proves it. A row is added only together with its test. The build runs `FailureTableCheck` after the integration tests, and it fails unless every test linked from a table in this file ran and passed in the same build.

[design.md](design.md) lists the rows the finished service has to prove, in sections 3 and 13. A row here claims only the part of its design row that tests prove today.

- Rows 1 to 5, 8, 13, 15, 18 to 20, 24, 26 to 28 and 31 are the whole design rows.
- Rows 1 to 4, 8, 20 and 26 are proven with replicas that run as processes of their own: the packaged jar in a child JVM, with the configuration that ships and the allowance for the local receiver. A kill is a SIGKILL of such a process and a shutdown is a SIGTERM. The replica inside the test JVM only takes the requests that set those tests up. Its worker does not run, so a delivery of such a test is claimed by a process and by nothing else.
  - No kill is landed by timing. For rows 2, 3 and 4 the test holds a lock that the next statement of the replica needs, waits until `pg_stat_activity` shows the replica waiting for it, kills the replica and lets go of the lock only when the process is gone. For row 1 the receiver holds the request. The blocked statement is a read or lies inside a transaction, so the backend that outlives its replica commits nothing. The test waits until that backend has left before it asserts what the kill left behind.
  - A blocked statement fails when the lock timeout of its pool is over, after 2 s in the API and after 5 s in the worker. The test has that long to see the replica waiting. A run that missed the wait would fail and not pass.
  - A held request is cut at the attempt deadline of 15 s. Rows 1, 8 and 20 act while the receiver holds the requests of a replica, and have that long for it: row 1 kills the replica, row 8 stops the container, and row 20 sends the SIGTERM, waits for `poller_stopped` and asks the readiness probe. A run that took longer would fail and not pass: a request that was cut is recorded as a failed attempt, where row 1 shows no attempt row and rows 8 and 20 show a `success` for each request that was held. Rows 2 and 26 hold a request only while the test takes its lock on the delivery row.
  - In row 8 the receiver holds its eight requests until the container has stopped, and answers them then. Two limits apply. The eight requests have to arrive and the container has to stop inside the 15 s of the attempt deadline. The eight results then have to be recorded before the replicas give the finalize up, 55 s after their claims began, so stopping and starting the container together have to fit into that time. A run that took longer than those 55 s would fail and not pass as well: the deliveries would be claimed again and sent a second time. Every answer to the event that is sent while the database is down, and again until it is back, is a 503, and the answer after that is a 202 for a new event.
  - In row 20 the readiness probe of the replica answers `OUT_OF_SERVICE` while the four requests are held and the poller has stopped, so the worker drains before the web server stops. That the process ended before a lease ran out is read from the four deliveries: each keeps the end of its lease as its due time, and that time still lies ahead on the database clock when the process is gone.
  - In row 26 the replica is cut off while it is seen waiting in its claim, or in the first statement of its finalize transaction. The first test finds the read that timed out in the output of the replica. After the 15 s of the socket timeout the replica still has to pass over the other connections of its pool, one aliveness test of 1 s each, before a claim gets a connection that was opened after the cut ended. The second test waits until the server has ended the transaction and does not measure how long that took. The limit of 10 s is what `DatabasePoolsIT` reads from a live session of each pool.
- Row 14 leaves out that the healthy endpoints of the tenant keep a closed circuit. No circuit opens yet, so no test could show one staying closed. What is proven is what would open it: such an endpoint gets no failed attempt.
- Rows 13 to 15 and 27 hold requests or lookups open and assert while they are. A held lookup stays stuck until the test lets go of it. A held request is cut at the attempt deadline of 15 s, so those tests have that long, and a run that took longer would fail and not pass: the replica would by then have sent the requests that the test shows it does not send.
- Rows 6, 7 and 12 are proven inside one test JVM.
  - The four claimers of row 6 are pollers in the test JVM, each with a worker and a replica id of its own, on one database.
  - In row 7 the claim that takes the delivery over is made by the replica that made the first claim. The fence does not know which replica holds a token, only whether the token is still the lease.
  - The delivery of row 12 kills no worker. The test claims it twelve times and lets each lease run out, which leaves the delivery as twelve killed workers would.
- Row 11 leaves out that its endpoint is healthy. In the design, other deliveries to the endpoint succeed between the attempts, and the circuit stays closed. No circuit opens yet, so the service cannot tell an endpoint that rejects one event from an endpoint that is down. The endpoint of the test answers 500 to every request until the delivery is dead, and 200 after that.
- Row 9 is provisional. Rows 9 and 10 of the design are about an endpoint that is down, and what they prove is the circuit breaker: the circuit opens, the deliveries are held and probed, and a recovery drains them. Without a breaker, such an endpoint gets all twelve attempts of a delivery. Row 9 claims what holds today: the delivery is dead-lettered and a replay delivers it, and a delivery that is still pending after 72 hours is dead-lettered. It is replaced by the two rows of the design when the circuit breaker exists.
  - The expiry job of the replica does not run by itself in `ExpiryIT#pendingPastBudgetBecomesDead`. The test calls each run, so what a delivery is after a run is what that run made of it.

The kill table has rows K1 to K5. K6 to K9 are not in it yet. K6 is a kill inside a probe and K7 a kill inside a bulk replay, and neither the circuit breaker nor the bulk replay exists. K8 and K9, a replica killed while it creates an endpoint or a tenant, are row 29 of the design and are not proven yet. What is tested today is those two transactions failing without a kill, in `AdminApiIT#failedBucketInsertLeavesNoTenant` and `EndpointApiIT#failedCircuitInsertLeavesNoEndpoint`.

## Kill table

Each row is a SIGKILL of a real replica process, landed at a chosen point.

| # | SIGKILL lands | State left | Recovery | Visible effect | Test |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | In the ingest transaction, before commit | Nothing; Postgres rolls back | Producer retries with the same key | One event, one delivery per endpoint | `IngestKillIT#killedBeforeCommit` |
| K2 | After ingest commit, before the response | Event and deliveries committed | Retry returns the original event id with `Idempotent-Replay: true` | One event | `IngestKillIT#killedAfterCommitBeforeResponse` |
| K3 | After claim commit, inside the load of an ordinary closed-circuit delivery, before any byte is sent | Row leased, attempt consumed, no request | Lease expires, another replica reclaims | Attempt 1 logged `unknown`; subscriber sees one request | `WorkerKillIT#killedAfterClaimBeforeSend` |
| K4 | Mid-request | Row leased; subscriber may have processed | Same as K3 | Up to two requests, same `webhook-id` | `WorkerKillIT#killedMidRequest` |
| K5 | After the subscriber's 2xx, before finalize commits | Row leased; subscriber has processed | Same as K3 | Exactly two requests, same `webhook-id`; one `succeeded` | `WorkerKillIT#killedAfterResponseBeforeCommit` |

- K2 is state-equivalent to a lost response, and no signal can be landed between a commit and the response that follows it. The test sends the request and throws the answer away, reads from the database that the event is committed, kills the replica and sends the request again to another one.
- In K4 the request of the first attempt has arrived when the kill comes, because the receiver holds it. The subscriber therefore sees two requests.
- A SIGKILL during graceful drain is K4 or K5.
- **Orphaned statements.** Killing a replica does not cancel the statement its backend is running. That statement may still execute, and an autocommit statement may still commit. Safety relies on the lease token and on every autocommit write being harmless when orphaned, not on the statement having stopped. The autocommit writes that exist today are the release, the endpoint patch and the key rotation. An orphaned release gives a delivery back that nobody is sending, which the next claim takes. An orphaned endpoint patch sets the event types the tenant asked for, and repeating the call changes nothing. A rotation whose response was lost leaves a key that nobody holds, and the operator repeats the call. No proof of this table blocks an autocommit write: a kill is landed on a read or inside a transaction.
